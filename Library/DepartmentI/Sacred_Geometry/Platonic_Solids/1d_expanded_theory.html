Which shapes fill space without leaving gaps?

Zero-Math Disclaimer

Never studied geometry? Perfect. This guide assumes you know nothing. We start with tiling a bathroom floor and end up understanding why the universe stacks certain shapes and not others — all in plain English.

Some shapes stack together perfectly, like bricks in a wall. Others always leave awkward gaps, no matter how you turn them. This chapter is the story of why — first on a flat floor, then in solid 3D space.

01

What "Tessellation" Means

You already know this idea — you have walked on it.

A tessellation (say "tess-uh-LAY-shun") is just a way of covering a surface with shapes so that there are no gaps and no overlaps. The shapes fit together like puzzle pieces that tile forever in every direction.

When every tile is the same regular shape (all sides equal, all corners equal), we call it a regular tessellation. Those are the ones we chase in this lesson.

Simply Put: Bathroom Tiles

Picture the tiled floor of a bathroom or kitchen. The tiles cover the whole floor — no bare patches, no tiles piled on top of each other. That is a tessellation. Square tiles are the classic choice because squares fit perfectly. Now ask: could the tiler have used regular pentagon tiles instead? The surprising answer is no — and there is a beautiful reason why.

02

The Flat Floor: Only Three Tiles Work

The whole secret is one number: 360.

Here is the key idea. Look at any single corner point where tiles meet. Walking all the way around that point is one full turn — exactly 360 degrees. For tiles to meet perfectly, the corner angles crowded around that point must add up to exactly 360°. Too little and you get a gap; too much and they overlap.

Every regular shape has a fixed interior angle (the angle at each of its corners). The formula is:

$$\text{interior angle} = \frac{(n-2)\times 180°}{n} \quad \text{for an } n\text{-sided shape}$$

A shape tiles the flat plane only when its interior angle divides evenly into 360°. Let's check the candidates:

Simply Put: Three Winners, Forever

Only the triangle, the square, and the hexagon ever tile a flat floor by themselves. Once shapes get to seven or more sides, their corners are so wide that only two fit around a point — leaving a permanent gap. That is the entire reason floor tiles come as squares and honeycomb hexagons, never as pentagons.

04

Stepping Up to 3D: Dihedral Angles

The corner becomes an edge, and the angle gets a fancy name.

In 3D, solids don't meet at a single point — they meet along a shared edge (a line where two faces come together). The angle that matters now is the dihedral angle: the angle between the two flat faces that hinge along that edge, like the angle of an open book.

The rule is exactly the same idea as before, just lifted one dimension: for solids to pack around a shared edge with no gaps, their dihedral angles must add up to 360°. Here are the dihedral angles of all five Platonic solids:

Where do these numbers come from? (optional)

You don't need to derive them, but for the curious: each dihedral angle comes from a short piece of trigonometry on the solid's faces. For example, the cube's is obviously $90°$ (two walls of a room). The tetrahedron's is $\arccos(1/3) \approx 70.53°$, and the octahedron's is its supplement, $\arccos(-1/3) \approx 109.47°$. Notice those two are partners — that partnership is exactly what makes the tet–oct honeycomb click together in Chapter 06.

Simply Put: The Open-Book Angle

Open a book partway and look at it edge-on. The angle the two covers make is a dihedral angle. Every solid has a built-in "book angle" wherever two of its faces meet. To stack solids around a shared line without gaps, those book angles have to add up to a full circle — 360°.

05

Why the Cube Stands Alone

One solid, one clean division.

Scan the "360° ÷ angle" column above. Only one row lands on a whole number: the cube, with $360 \div 90 = 4$. Four cubes meet exactly around every edge, and they stack outward forever into a flawless 3D grid. This is why sugar cubes, dice, salt crystals, and children's blocks all pack so neatly.

Every other Platonic solid leaves a fractional remainder — a sliver of empty space that can never be closed, no matter how you rotate or shift the pieces. The regular tetrahedron is the famous "surprise": people long assumed it tiled space (even Aristotle claimed it did), but $5 \times 70.53° = 352.65°$ falls just short of 360°, leaving a thin gap.

✓ The Cube Fills Space

90° × 4 = 360°

Four cubes close every edge exactly. Stack them and 3D space is filled with zero waste — the only Platonic solid that can.

✗ The Tetrahedron Falls Short

70.53° × 5 = 352.65°

Five tetrahedra around an edge leave a 7.35° wedge gap. A sixth would overlap. So it cannot tile space alone.

06

The Tet–Oct Honeycomb

Two shapes that fail alone succeed as a team.

Here is the magic. The tetrahedron ($70.53°$) and octahedron ($109.47°$) each fail alone — but their angles are partners. Put two tetrahedra and two octahedra around a shared edge and watch:

$$70.53°\times 2 + 109.47°\times 2 = 360°$$

The gap closes exactly. Alternating these two solids builds the tetrahedral–octahedral honeycomb (also called the alternated cubic honeycomb). It is rigid, gap-free, and shows up in crystal structures, architecture (space-frame trusses), and the S.P.I.R.E. engine's preferred 3D lattice.

Simply Put: Teamwork

Think of two puzzle pieces that are useless on their own but snap together perfectly. Tetrahedra and octahedra are shaped so their leftover gaps are exactly each other's size. Together they fill space like a flawless 3D brick wall.

07

Curious Asides

The Platonic solids are just the beginning. Space-filling gets wonderfully strange:

The truncated octahedron DOES tile

Slice the corners off an octahedron and you get a 14-faced solid that fills space all by itself. Kelvin proposed it in 1887 as the most efficient way to divide space into equal cells.

Weaire–Phelan foam beats Kelvin

In 1993, Weaire and Phelan found a two-cell foam pattern with 0.3% less surface area than Kelvin's cells — the best known way to partition space into equal-volume bubbles. It clad the "Water Cube" at the 2008 Olympics.

Voronoi cells & nature's foams

Soap bubbles, dried mud, giraffe spots, and cells in tissue all form Voronoi tessellations: each region is the area closest to one seed point. Nature tiles space to minimise energy, not to be tidy.

08

Data Dictionary

Tessellation Tiling
Covering a surface (or space) with shapes so there are no gaps and no overlaps. A tiled floor is a 2D tessellation.
Interior angle
The angle at each corner of a polygon. For a regular n-gon it equals (n−2)×180°/n.
Dihedral angle
In 3D, the angle between two flat faces that meet along a shared edge — like the angle of an open book.
Honeycomb
A tessellation of 3D space by solid cells. The tet–oct honeycomb alternates tetrahedra and octahedra.
Space-filling
A shape (or set of shapes) that can tile all of 3D space with no gaps. The cube is the only space-filling Platonic solid.
Vertex figure
The little shape you get by "slicing off" a corner of a tiling — it records exactly which cells and angles meet at that point or edge.
Voronoi tessellation
A tiling where each cell is the region closest to a given seed point. Common in nature and foams.
