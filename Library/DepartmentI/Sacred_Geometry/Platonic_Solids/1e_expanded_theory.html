How a computer keeps a shape in its memory

Zero-Math Disclaimer

No coding experience needed. We will show that a 3D shape, inside a computer, is really just two ordinary lists of numbers — one for the corners, one for the recipes that connect them. That's the whole secret.

In sub-lesson 1A you learned a shape is made of dots, lines and surfaces. In 1B you learned the magic checksum $V-E+F=2$. Now we answer the practical question: when the S.P.I.R.E. Engine "holds" a cube, what is actually sitting in its memory? The answer is refreshingly simple.

01

From Dots to Buffers

A "buffer" is just a fancy word for a list of numbers.

Back in 1A we said any solid can be described by its skin — its corners, edges and flat faces. Programmers call storing "just the skin" a boundary representation, or B-rep. When it comes time to actually save that skin in a computer, it becomes two plain lists:

  • a list of every corner and where it sits, and
  • a list of every face and which corners it joins.

Each of these lists is called a buffer. A buffer is nothing scary — it is literally a numbered row of values, like a spreadsheet column. Two buffers, and you have a shape.

Simply Put: A Dot-to-Dot Book

Remember dot-to-dot puzzles? One page lists numbered dots printed at fixed spots. The instructions say "draw from 1 to 2 to 3." The computer does exactly this: one list places the numbered dots, the other list says which dots to join. Nothing more.

02

The Vertex Table — a GPS List

Where every corner lives.

The first buffer is the vertex buffer. It is a numbered list of GPS addresses. Row 0 holds the coordinates of corner 0, row 1 holds corner 1, and so on. Every corner appears exactly once. Its row number is its permanent name — its index.

Simply Put: A Contacts List

Think of the phone contacts on your phone. Each contact has a name and a number, and you never store the same person twice. The vertex buffer is the shape's contacts list: each corner stored once, with a fixed position, referred to later just by its slot.

03

The Face Table — Recipe Cards

Which corners to join, and why we point instead of copy.

The second buffer is the index buffer (also called the face buffer). Each entry is a small recipe: a list of corner numbers to connect into one face. It never writes coordinates — only the slot numbers from the vertex list. A cube has six square faces, so six recipe cards:

facecorner acorner bcorner ccorner d
0 · bottom0154
1 · top3762
2 · front4567
3 · back0321
4 · right1265
5 · left0473

Why point at a corner instead of copying it?

Corner 6 shows up on the top, front and right faces. If each face copied out the full coordinates, that one corner would be written three times. Multiply across a whole model and you waste memory — and worse, if you nudge the corner you must remember to fix all three copies or the shape tears open. By storing the number 6 instead, every face shares the one true corner. Fix it once, fixed everywhere.

Simply Put: Don't Re-type the Address

When three friends live at the same house, you don't paste the full street address next to each name — you just say "same as above." If the family moves, you update one address, not three. Indexing is the mesh saying "same corner as slot 6" instead of re-typing where slot 6 is.

04

The Half-Edge Structure

A smarter address book for walking the surface.

Two buffers are great for drawing a shape, but clumsy for exploring it. Suppose you're standing on one face and want to step to the face right next door. With only the two lists you'd have to search through every recipe to find the neighbour. Slow.

So engines add a third structure: the half-edge (its formal name is a doubly-connected edge list). The idea: take every edge and split it into two one-way arrows pointing opposite directions. Each arrow — a "half-edge" — remembers four things:

  • origin — the corner it starts from,
  • twin — its partner arrow going the other way,
  • next — the arrow after it around the same face,
  • face — the face it hugs.

The shared edge becomes two twin arrows. Cross a twin and you're on the neighbouring face — instantly.

Simply Put: A Revolving Door

Picture a revolving door between two rooms. Each pane is a one-way arrow: follow the panes around and you circle one room (that's next, walking a face). Step through to the pane facing you and you're in the room next door (that's twin, crossing to the neighbouring face). You never need a map of the whole building — just keep following doors.

05

Asking the Mesh Questions

These questions are called adjacency queries.

Once the half-edges are in place, questions about who-touches-what become tiny walks. In geometry the umbrella term for "what is next to what" is adjacency. Two common queries:

Walk around a face

Start at any half-edge, keep following next until you return home. Each hop is instant, so listing a face's corners costs only as many steps as it has sides.

h → next → next → … → back to h

Fan around a corner

To find every face meeting at one corner, alternate twin then next. You spin around the corner like the spokes of a wheel, touching each neighbouring face once.

h → twin → next → twin → next → …

Simply Put: Following Signposts

Instead of unfolding a giant map every time, you just read the signpost at your feet: "next corner this way," "neighbour that way." A few steps and you've answered the question — no searching the whole model.

06

Computing χ in Code

The health check that must equal 2.

Here's the payoff for storing things as buffers: Euler's checksum from 1B falls out of them almost for free. Count the vertex rows to get V. Count the face rows to get F. For edges, add up every face's number of sides and halve it — because each edge is shared by two faces and would otherwise be counted twice. Then check $V-E+F$ is 2. If it isn't, the mesh has a hole or a crack.

# pseudo-code: is this mesh watertight?
function eulerCheck(vertexBuffer, faceBuffer):
    V = length(vertexBuffer)          # count the corners
    F = length(faceBuffer)            # count the faces

    sideTotal = 0
    for face in faceBuffer:
        sideTotal += length(face)     # 3 for a triangle, 4 for a quad…

    E = sideTotal / 2                 # every edge shared by 2 faces

    chi = V - E + F
    if chi == 2:
        return "OK — watertight, genus-0 ✓"
    else:
        return "BROKEN — chi = " + chi   # a hole, crack, or stray vertex

08

Store the Seed, Not the Asset

And a word on memory footprint.

A Platonic solid is fully pinned down by a rule. So the engine often doesn't bother saving the buffers at all — it saves a tiny seed (which solid, how big, which way it faces) and rebuilds the buffers on demand. Same seed in, same shape out, every single time.

This is the procedural generation idea behind the rotating sigils around this Academy: a sigil isn't a saved picture, it's a small recipe replayed by a generator. Store the recipe, bake the picture fresh.

Simply Put: Text the Recipe

Mailing a photo of a cake is big and clumsy; texting the recipe is tiny, and anyone who follows it bakes the identical cake. The engine keeps the recipe.

Memory footprint, roughly

Storing an icosahedron three ways:

  • Triangle soup (60 copied verts)~720 B
  • Indexed (12 verts + 20 faces)~384 B
  • Seed ("icosa, scale 3")~8 B

Figures are illustrative (floats + ints). The point is the ratio: a recipe is orders of magnitude smaller than the baked mesh.

09

Words Worth Knowing

Vertex buffer
A numbered list storing each corner's $(x,y,z)$ position exactly once.
Index buffer face buffer
A list of faces, where each face is a group of corner numbers (indices), not coordinates.
B-rep boundary representation
Describing a solid only by its outer skin (corners, edges, faces), never its filled interior.
Half-edge
One of the two one-way arrows an edge is split into; it remembers its origin, twin, next, and face.
Twin
The partner half-edge running the opposite way along the same edge; crossing it lands you on the neighbouring face.
Adjacency
Which pieces touch which — e.g. the faces meeting at a corner. Answered by short half-edge walks.
Watertight / Manifold
A surface with no holes or cracks where every edge borders exactly two faces. A watertight closed solid has $\chi = 2$.
Core Architecture Directive

Two lists, one checksum, a seed

A shape in memory is a vertex buffer plus an index buffer. Add half-edges and you can walk it fast; add $V-E+F$ and you can prove it's whole; keep only the seed and you can carry it in a whisper.
