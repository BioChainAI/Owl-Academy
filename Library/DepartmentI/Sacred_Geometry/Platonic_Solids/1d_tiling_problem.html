The Tiling Problem in 3D Space

◆ Hub / 1A · Primitives 1B · Topology 1C · Resonance 1D · Tiling 1E · Engine 1F · Lab
DEEP DIVE: EXPANDED THEORY

Step 1 — The Flat Case: Why Only Three Tiles

Before we conquer 3D space, warm up in the plane. A regular tessellation is a way of covering an infinite flat floor using copies of one regular polygon, with no gaps and no overlaps. Astonishingly, out of the infinitely many regular polygons, exactly three can do it.

The rule is pure arithmetic. Where polygons meet at a shared corner, their interior angles must sum to a full turn, $360°$. So a regular $n$-gon tiles the plane only if its interior angle divides $360°$ evenly:

$$\text{Triangle } 60°\times 6 = 360° \quad\;\; \text{Square } 90°\times 4 = 360° \quad\;\; \text{Hexagon } 120°\times 3 = 360°$$

The regular pentagon fails: its interior angle is $108°$, and $360° \div 108° = 3.33\ldots$ — not a whole number. Three pentagons around a point leave a $360 - 324 = 36°$ wedge gap; four would overlap. No arrangement works.

Simply Put: The Bathroom Floor Test

Think of tiling a bathroom floor with one shape. Triangles work. Squares work. Hexagons work (that is why honeycomb and soccer-ball patterns show up everywhere). Try it with regular pentagons and you always get ugly gaps you would have to fill with grout. The corners just refuse to add up to a clean full turn.

Step 2 — The 3D Rule: Angles Around a Shared Edge

In 3D the shared "corner" becomes a shared edge. When solids pack around a common edge, the dihedral angles — the angle between the two faces meeting at that edge — must sum to a full turn, $360°$, with no gap and no overlap. It is the exact same divisibility argument, lifted one dimension.

$$\sum_{\text{cells around an edge}} \theta_{\text{dihedral}} = 360°$$

Simply Put: The Slice-of-Pie Rule

Look at a shared edge end-on and each solid takes up a wedge, like a slice of pie. To wrap all the way around the edge with no leftover crust, your slice angles have to add up to a perfect $360°$. Only the cube's $90°$ slice divides evenly (four slices, done). Every other Platonic solid leaves a sliver of empty pie — or would need to overlap.

Step 3 — The Verdict: The Cube Stands Alone (But Friends Help)

Running the table gives a clean result: the cube is the only Platonic solid that fills 3-space by itself. Four cubes meet snugly around every edge ($90°\times 4 = 360°$), stacking into an infinite lattice with no gaps — exactly how table-salt crystals (NaCl) and children's building blocks pack.

But solids can team up. The star combination is the tetrahedral–octahedral honeycomb: alternate regular tetrahedra and octahedra so that around each shared edge you place two of each:

$$70.53°\times 2 \;+\; 109.47°\times 2 \;=\; 141.06° + 218.94° = 360°$$

The wedges lock together perfectly. This "tet–oct" lattice (also called the alternated cubic honeycomb) is one of nature's favourite scaffolds — and it is why the S.P.I.R.E. engine reaches for the tet–oct packing when it needs a rigid, gap-free 3D grid.

Real-world aside

Chasing the best way to fill space is a live problem. For equal-sized bubbles, Lord Kelvin's truncated-octahedron cells were the champion for a century — until the Weaire–Phelan foam (1993) beat them with slightly less surface area. That foam's pattern was famously used for the "Water Cube" swimming venue at the 2008 Beijing Olympics.
